# Blog 6 - On College Math and the Cultures of Mathematics (10/6/2026)

> La mer qui monte.

Sorry that I haven't posted here for awhile, I've been busy with schoolwork. 

Anyways, I wanted to discuss today about "college level" math and why it is "difficult". In particular, I don't think a lot of these topics are actually *that* difficult. In fact, in some ways, they might be easier than "high school" math. Before we begin, I want to note that of course there are very difficult college level courses in mathematics (or else the field of mathematics wouldn't exist). I'm mainly going to be addressing the introductory mathematics courses in college (like Real Analysis).

Alright, suppose that you are a fairly advanced math kid in high school. Let's say that you are really doing everything you can at your high school. Usually, this looks like:

- Taking AP Calculus BC, maybe Multivariable Calculus if your high school offers it.
- Taking the AMC and related competitions. 
- Joining a math honor society.

Call this person Alice. Alice has certainly accomplished a lot in high school, no doubt. Calculus is already a college course, and Multivariable Calculus is well into college territory. She also does the AMC, which involves lot of problem solving insights and applying a variety of techniques. Yet, she might (not everyone will, but a lot will) struggle a lot in a class like Real Analysis. How come?

Consider a typical problem in Calculus. Something like $$\displaystyle \int_{0}^{1} (x+2)^{2} \mathrm{d}x$$. One would make a u-substitution with $$u = x+2$$ and then integrate accordingly. Consider a Real Analysis problem, which might be something closer to "Prove that $$f(x)=x^{2}$$ is Riemann integrable on $$[0,1]$$". These seem to be pretty similar in nature, but the solutions are vastly different. The main point of contention is the word "Prove", although I believe that there is a deeper discrepancy at play here.

In mathematics, it is often regarded that there are two "cultures" of mathematics: problem solvers and theory builders. Dr. Timothy Gowers has an amazing essay on this topic, and I recommend you read it first (The Two Cultures of Mathematics). To summarize, there are two cultures of mathematicians in which one concerns itself with solving problems and the other for understanding mathematics. Largely, grade school mathematics concerns itself with "solving problems". It should be noted that I am in no way saying that research level "problem solving" resembles anything similar to school math, I am just saying that if I must categorize school math it would go into the "problem solving" box.

I believe that classes such as Real Analysis are the first steps for young mathematicians to be exposed to the "theory building" side of things, which involves heavy levels of abstractions, generalizations, and rigor. Real Analysis might be regarded as an easy theory building class, even though it is considered "hard" by regular standards. Alice might struggle because she has not been exposed to the theory building culture, a culture that involves a different mindset than the problem solving one. She might be able to integrate crazy functions, but have little experience in writing a basic proof (the proof curriculum supplied in geometry classes is NOT sufficent whatsoever). This isn't her fault, and in time she will become an experienced and seasoned mathematician who will tackle much more difficult courses. But these courses might feel easier to her, for she is no longer adjusting to a completely different paradigm of thought.
